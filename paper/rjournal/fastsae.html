Fastsae: High-Performance Frequentist and Bayesian Small Area Estimation in R

Small area estimation (SAE) plays a pivotal operational role in official statistics, enabling the production of reliable disaggregated socioeconomic, health, and demographic indicators when domain-specific sample sizes are small or zero. However, practitioners in national statistical offices (NSOs) face a triple computational and operational barrier: frequentist spatial and spatio-temporal models with bootstrap mean squared error (MSE) estimation suffer from severe memory bottlenecks and slow runtimes in interpreted languages; non-Gaussian indicators (such as rates, skewed expenditures, and counts) traditionally require computationally demanding Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms that impede routine production; and model estimates often fail to aggregate to published higher-level totals, violating institutional consistency. We introduce fastsae, an R package engineered for high-performance frequentist and Bayesian small area estimation. On the frequentist side, fastsae implements optimized C++17 routines via RcppArmadillo paired with OpenMP multi-threading, accelerating the classical Fay-Herriot, spatial Fay-Herriot, spatio-temporal Fay-Herriot, and unit-level Battese-Harter-Fuller models alongside their analytical and bootstrap MSE estimators. On the Bayesian side, fastsae provides a unified empirical best prediction (EBP) interface powered by Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (INLA) across six distribution families (Gaussian, Beta, Gamma, Binomial, Poisson, Negative Binomial). Crucially, fastsae introduces exact survey dispersion mappings that respect design-based direct sampling variances and accommodates modern spatial priors (BYM, scaled BYM2, Leroux CAR, and SLM) as well as Knorr-Held space-time interaction dynamics. To bridge estimation and dissemination, the package features a two-stage hierarchical benchmarking engine for simultaneous provincial and national calibration, an automated reliability classification engine aligned with NSO standards, multi-model concordances, automated ggplot2 spatial mapping, and multi-sheet spreadsheet exports. Extensive benchmarks and finite population Monte Carlo simulations demonstrate multi-fold computational speedups (up to 364x for Fay-Herriot, 80x for Spatial Fay-Herriot, and 544x for spatio-temporal Beta) and peak RAM reductions up to 99.9% without loss of inferential precision.

In modern evidence-based governance, policy interventions are increasingly decentralized. Monitoring the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), allocating municipal fiscal transfers, targeting social safety net subsidies, and responding to localized disease outbreaks all require dependable statistical indicators at highly disaggregated geographic administrative levels . However, national statistical offices (NSOs) face structural sample size limitations. Large-scale household surveys—such as the American Community Survey (ACS) in the United States, the European Union Statistics on Income and Living Conditions (EU-SILC), and the National Socioeconomic Survey (Susenas) in Indonesia—are powered to yield acceptable sampling errors only at national or broad regional levels. When partitioned into sub-domains (e.g., municipalities, districts, or health zones), domain-specific sample sizes become sparse or zero, causing direct survey estimators to suffer from high sampling variance and unacceptable coefficients of variation .

Small area estimation (SAE) overcomes these limitations by ``borrowing strength’’ across domains and time periods via linking models that combine survey responses with auxiliary administrative registers, census aggregates, or geospatial layers . The cornerstone of this literature remains the area-level Fay-Herriot (FH) model , extended to account for spatial dependencies via Simultaneous Autoregressive (SAR) structures and temporal dynamics via panel autoregressive processes . In the presence of micro-level auxiliary data, the nested error regression model of (BHF) provides unit-level empirical best linear unbiased predictors (EBLUP).

Despite these theoretical advances, operationalizing small area models within production environments in NSOs has been hindered by three structural bottlenecks: %

As summarized in Table~\(\ref{tab:pkg_comparison}\), the current software landscape is compartmentalized. Foundational toolkits such as sae are implemented in interpreted R, lack multi-threading, and are restricted to Gaussian linear frameworks. Packages such as emdi offer visualizations but lack spatio-temporal dynamics and non-Gaussian spatial Bayesian likelihoods. Specialized packages such as tipsae implement Beta regression but depend on MCMC runtimes that scale poorly for panel graphs. Toolkits such as mcmcsae remain simulation-based, while SUMMER is tailored to demographic survey mortality rather than general survey design-variance matching.

To resolve these limitations, this paper introduces fastsae. Specifically, this paper makes four concrete, testable contributions: %

%% – Methodological Framework ————————————————-

Let \(m\) denote the number of small areas indexed by \(i = 1, \dots, m\). The classical area-level model is defined by: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_model} y_i = \bm{x}_i^\top \bm{\beta} + u_i + e_i, \quad u_i \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2), \quad e_i \overset{\mathrm{ind}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, D_i), \end{equation}\] % where \(y_i\) is the design-based direct estimator of the target indicator \(\theta_i\), \(\bm{x}_i\) is a \(p \times 1\) vector of auxiliary covariates, \(D_i\) is the known design variance, and \(u_i\) is the area random effect. The Empirical Best Linear Unbiased Predictor (EBLUP) is given by: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_eblup} \hat{\theta}_i = \hat{\gamma}_i y_i + (1 - \hat{\gamma}_i) \bm{x}_i^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}}, \quad \hat{\gamma}_i = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_u^2}{\hat{\sigma}_u^2 + D_i}, \end{equation}\] % where \(\hat{\bm{\beta}} = (\bm{X}^\top \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} \bm{X})^{-1} \bm{X}^\top \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} \bm{y}\) with \(\bm{V} = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_u^2 + D_1, \dots, \sigma_u^2 + D_m)\). Mean squared error is evaluated via the second-order Prasad-Rao approximation : % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_mse} \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_i) \approx g_{1i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2) + g_{2i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2) + 2 g_{3i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2). \end{equation}\]

To accommodate spatial dependence across contiguous domains, and modeled the area random effects \(\bm{u} = (u_1, \dots, u_m)^\top\) via a Simultaneous Autoregressive (SAR) process: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:sfh_sar} \bm{u} = \rho \bm{W} \bm{u} + \bm{\epsilon}, \quad \bm{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}_m(\bm{0}, \sigma_1^2 \bm{I}_m), \end{equation}\] % where \(\rho\) is the spatial autoregressive parameter and \(\bm{W}\) is a row-standardized spatial proximity matrix. The marginal covariance matrix is \(\bm{V}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) = \bm{G}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) + \bm{D}\), where \(\bm{G}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) = \sigma_1^2 [(\bm{I}_m - \rho \bm{W})^\top (\bm{I}_m - \rho \bm{W})]^{-1}\). The spatial EBLUP is: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:sfh_eblup} \hat{\bm{\theta}} = \bm{X} \hat{\bm{\beta}} + \hat{\bm{G}} \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} (\bm{y} - \bm{X} \hat{\bm{\beta}}). \end{equation}\] % Variance components are estimated via profile REML over the valid spectral bounds \(\rho \in (\lambda_{\min}^{-1}, \lambda_{\max}^{-1})\).

When small area data are collected across longitudinal waves \(t = 1, \dots, T\), borrowing strength across space and time yields substantial efficiency gains : % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:stfh_model} y_{it} = \bm{x}_{it}^\top \bm{\beta} + u_{1,it} + u_{2,it} + e_{it}, \end{equation}\] % where \(\bm{u}_{1,t} = \rho_1 \bm{W} \bm{u}_{1,t} + \bm{\epsilon}_{1,t}\) represents a spatial SAR process and \(u_{2,it} = \rho_2 u_{2,i,t-1} + \epsilon_{2,it}\) follows an AR(1) temporal process with \(|\rho_2| < 1\). In fastsae, the block-structured covariance matrix is assembled and inverted using optimized Kronecker routines.

When unit microdata are accessible alongside population auxiliary totals, fastsae implements the nested error regression model : % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:bhf_model} y_{dj} = \bm{x}_{dj}^\top \bm{\beta} + u_d + e_{dj}, \quad d = 1, \dots, D, \quad j = 1, \dots, n_d, \end{equation}\] % yielding EBLUP predictors \(\hat{\bar{Y}}_d = f_d \bar{y}_d + (1 - f_d)(\bar{\bm{X}}_{d, r}^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}} + \hat{\gamma}_d (\bar{y}_d - \bar{\bm{x}}_d^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}}))\), where \(f_d = n_d / N_d\).

For non-Gaussian indicators, fastsae formulates small area estimation within the Latent Gaussian Model (LGM) framework : % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:lgm_formulation} y_i \mid \eta_i, \bm{\theta} \sim \pi(y_i \mid \eta_i, \bm{\theta}), \quad \eta_i = g(\mu_i) = \bm{x}_i^\top \bm{\beta} + u_i + v_i, \end{equation}\] % where \(g(\cdot)\) is a monotone link function, \(v_i \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \tau_v^{-1})\) is an unstructured effect, and \(\bm{u} \sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0}, \bm{Q}^{-1}(\bm{\theta}_s))\) is a spatial Gaussian Markov Random Field (GMRF). By default, fastsae utilizes the Full Laplace approximation () rather than the simplified Laplace expansion, guaranteeing high analytical fidelity even near boundary domains.

To preserve design-based sampling properties in a Bayesian GLMM, fastsae injects distribution-specific dispersion parameters: %

The spatial random field \(\bm{u}\) supports five graph topologies: Besag ICAR , scaled BYM2 with Penalized Complexity (PC) priors , Leroux CAR with exact precision \(\bm{Q} = \tau[(1 - \rho)\bm{I} + \rho(\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}) - \bm{W})]\) , and the Spatial Lag Model (SLM). For panel observations across \(T \ge 2\), temporal trends support random walks (RW1, RW2) and AR(1) dynamics. Space-time interactions can be specified as additive, domain-specific, separable, or according to the four interaction types (\(\bm{Q}_{st} = \bm{Q}_s \otimes \bm{Q}_t\)).

In official statistics, model-based small area estimates \(\hat{\theta}_{di}\) for domain \(i\) in province/region \(g\) must aggregate to officially published higher-level targets . Let \(w_{di}\) denote domain population weights such that \(W_g = \sum_{i \in g} w_{di}\) is the provincial total weight, and \(W_{\text{nat}} = \sum_g W_g\) is the national total weight. Let \(T_g\) denote the provincial target and \(T_{\text{nat}}\) denote the national aggregate target.

fastsae implements a two-stage hierarchical benchmarking calibration: % % Across both stages, fastsae supports four calibration mechanisms: %

NSOs classify domain estimates into publication tiers based on the Relative Standard Error (\(\mathrm{RSE} = \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_i)}}{|\hat{\theta}_i|} \times 100\%\)). fastsae provides implementing institutional thresholding: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:reliability} \mathrm{Tier}_i = \begin{cases} \text{Reliable (Direct Publish)}, & \mathrm{RSE}_i < c_1, \\ \text{Use with Caution (Accompanying Warning)}, & c_1 \le \mathrm{RSE}_i < c_2, \\ \text{Unreliable (Suppress or Aggregate)}, & \mathrm{RSE}_i \ge c_2, \end{cases} \end{equation}\] % where default thresholds \((c_1, c_2) = (20\%, 30\%)\) or \((25\%, 50\%)\) conform to standards adopted by agencies such as BPS-Statistics Indonesia and Eurostat.

%% – Computational Benchmarks ————————————————-

We evaluated computational speed and peak memory consumption across domain sizes \(D \in \{30, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000\}\) using simulated datasets with 5 auxiliary covariates. Benchmarks were conducted using the bench package on an Apple Silicon processor under R~4.4.2.

Table~\(\ref{tab:bench_freq}\) demonstrates the dramatic scalability gains of fastsae. At \(D=1,000\), classical FH completes in 4 milliseconds in fastsae compared to 1.5 seconds in sae (\(364\times\) slower) and 51 seconds in emdi (\(12,343\times\) slower). For spatio-temporal models at \(D=1,000\), sae required nearly 30 minutes (1,786 seconds) and consumed 32.5 gigabytes of RAM, whereas fastsae finished in 58 seconds using under 1 megabyte of memory. : fastsae eliminates out-of-memory bottlenecks, enabling routine spatio-temporal estimation across thousands of domains on standard laptop hardware.

For bounded rates, we benchmarked against tipsae using Stan (4 chains, 2,000 iterations).

As shown in Table~\(\ref{tab:bench_beta}\), deterministic Laplace approximations in fastsae scale flatly with domain count: fitting a spatial Beta model at \(D=1,000\) requires only 1.73 seconds in fastsae compared to 404 seconds in tipsae (\(234\times\) speedup). In spatio-temporal panel models (\(D=50, T=5\)), tipsae required 15.1 minutes (908 seconds), whereas fastsae completed in 1.67 seconds (\(544\times\) speedup). Despite this speed differential, inferential concordance on \(D=1,000\) yields Pearson correlation \(r = 0.9993\), Spearman \(\rho = 0.9992\), and Mean Absolute Difference \(\mathrm{MAD} = 0.0197\). : Deterministic Laplace approximation in fastsae achieves inferential equivalence with fully converged Hamiltonian MCMC in seconds rather than minutes or hours.

To assess frequentist design properties, we conducted design-based Monte Carlo simulations under known ground truth at national scale (\(D = 500\) small areas, \(R = 25\) independent sample draws) using a synthetic finite population of \(N = 250,000\) individuals.

Table~\(\ref{tab:mc_truth}\) demonstrates that reduces empirical RMSE from 0.0719 (direct survey estimator) to 0.0481, yielding a \(1.92\times\) empirical efficiency gain. Relative Root Mean Squared Error drops from \(27.1\%\) to \(19.1\%\). Comparing Bayesian engines, fastsae achieves a slightly lower empirical RMSE than tipsae (0.0481 vs. 0.0487) and produces narrower credible intervals (0.173 vs. 0.202) while requiring nearly \(9\times\) less runtime per fit. : fastsae produces well-calibrated, highly efficient area-level predictions that closely track true finite-population parameters while scaling effortlessly to national-scale administrative systems.

%% – Applied Illustration —————————————————–

We demonstrate an end-to-end NSO production workflow using the benchmark dataset (\(D = 42\) domains across 6 provincial groups).

library("fastsae")
data("sim_area")
sim_area$prov <- rep(paste0("Prov_", 1:6), each = 7)
sim_area$pop <- round(runif(nrow(sim_area), 15000, 45000))

# Construct row-standardized spatial proximity matrix
coords <- cbind(sim_area$x_coord, sim_area$y_coord)
dists <- as.matrix(dist(coords))
W <- (dists < quantile(dists, 0.18)) * 1
diag(W) <- 0
rs <- rowSums(W); rs[rs == 0] <- 1; W_std <- W / rs

# Fit Frequentist and Bayesian Models
fit_fh <- eblup_fh(y_gaussian ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  data = sim_area, method = "REML", print_result = FALSE)
fit_sfh <- eblup_sfh(y_gaussian ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  domain = "area", W = W_std, data = sim_area,
  method = "REML", print_result = FALSE)
fit_beta <- hb_area(y_beta ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  data = sim_area, family = "beta", spatial = "bym2",
  W = W, print_result = FALSE)

%% – Summary and Discussion —————————————————

Small area estimation in official statistics requires reconciling mathematical rigor with strict computational and operational constraints. fastsae resolves these challenges by bridging high-performance compiled linear algebra, deterministic Bayesian approximations, and production-ready dissemination tools.

As synthesized in Table~\(\ref{tab:synthesis}\), fastsae provides substantial efficiency gains across both frequentist and Bayesian paradigms. Future developments will focus on expanding unit-level spatial EBP under non-Gaussian likelihoods, integrating zero-inflated Beta (ZIB) specifications for extreme sparse indicators, and supporting direct spatial polygon streaming.
