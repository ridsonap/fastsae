fastsae: Frequentist and Bayesian Small Area Estimation in R

Small area estimation (SAE) combines survey data with auxiliary information to estimate parameters of domains whose samples are too small for direct estimation. We present fastsae, an R package that brings the main area-level and unit-level models of official statistics together in one interface. The frequentist part fits the Fay-Herriot model, its spatial and spatio-temporal extensions, the Battese-Harter-Fuller unit-level model and the two-fold nested error model, with analytical and bootstrap mean squared errors, in compiled code that never forms a dense inverse of the marginal covariance matrix. The hierarchical Bayes part fits area-level, unit-level and two-fold models with Gaussian, beta, gamma, binomial, Poisson and negative binomial likelihoods and spatial, temporal and space-time random effects, by integrated nested Laplace approximations or, through the same call, by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in JAX. For beta, gamma and negative binomial data the dispersion of each area is fixed by the design-based sampling variance of its direct estimate, and the default priors were chosen by simulation and are documented. Further functions benchmark estimates to published totals at one or two levels, classify them by relative standard error, compare models, and produce maps and spreadsheet exports. We describe the methods and their implementation, compare runtimes and estimates with the packages sae, emdi and tipsae, report a design-based simulation, and illustrate a complete workflow with poverty rates of the health districts of Emilia-Romagna.

1 Introduction

Official statistics are increasingly asked for indicators at levels of geography or population detail that national surveys were not designed for: poverty rates of districts, unemployment of municipalities, or health indicators of subregions (Pfeffermann 2013; Rao and Molina 2015). Direct estimates from such domains rest on few observations and have large sampling variances. Small area estimation (SAE) reduces these variances by linking the domains through a model with auxiliary information, at the area level when only domain aggregates are available (Fay and Herriot 1979) and at the unit level when auxiliary variables are known for the sampled units and as population means (Battese et al. 1988). The basic models have been extended to spatially correlated area effects (Petrucci and Salvati 2006; Pratesi and Salvati 2008), to repeated surveys (Marhuenda et al. 2013), and, for proportions, rates and counts, to non-Gaussian likelihoods, either through empirical best predictors of generalized linear mixed models (Boubeta et al. 2016) or by hierarchical Bayes (HB) estimation, which also accounts for the uncertainty about the variance parameters (Fabrizi et al. 2011; Rao and Molina 2015; Janicki 2020).

Producing small area statistics routinely raises three practical requirements. First, the methods must scale to the thousands of areas, and the repeated fits of bootstrap mean squared error (MSE) estimation, of national production systems. Second, the HB models that suit bounded and count indicators should not require each analyst to write and diagnose an MCMC sampler. Third, published small area estimates are usually benchmarked to direct estimates of larger domains and accompanied by indicators of their reliability, so these steps belong in the same workflow as the model fit.

Several R packages implement parts of this (Table 1). sae (Molina and Marhuenda 2015) covers the frequentist area-level models, including spatial and spatio-temporal Fay-Herriot models, and the unit-level model in R code. emdi (Kreutzmann et al. 2019) adds transformations, the empirical best predictor for poverty indicators, benchmarking to a national total, maps and spreadsheet export. hbsae (Boonstra 2022) fits Gaussian area- and unit-level HB models by numerical integration over the variance parameter and benchmarks estimates to any set of linear constraints. tipsae (De Nicolò and Gardini 2024) fits beta-type area-level models with spatial and temporal effects in Stan, mcmcsae (Boonstra 2026) fits a wide class of multilevel models by MCMC, and SUMMER (Li et al. 2019; Li et al. 2025) fits area- and cluster-level models with INLA, focusing on demographic and health surveys.

fastsae covers these models and steps in one interface and was written with the first two requirements in mind. Its contributions are:

  1. Compiled implementations of the Fay-Herriot, spatial, spatio-temporal, unit-level and two-fold models with analytical and bootstrap MSEs that never form a dense inverse of the marginal covariance matrix, and run the bootstrap in parallel with results that do not depend on the number of threads.
  2. One HB interface, hb_area(), hb_unit() and hb_twofold(), for six likelihoods and spatial, temporal and space-time effects, fitted by INLA (Rue et al. 2009) or by the No-U-Turn sampler of NumPyro (Hoffman and Gelman 2014; Phan et al. 2019) through interchangeable engine objects. For beta, gamma and negative binomial data the dispersion of each area is fixed by the sampling variance of its direct estimate, and the default priors are documented together with the simulations that led to them.
  3. Post-estimation functions for benchmarking at one or two levels, reliability classification, model comparison, maps and export, which accept the output of every model.

The remainder of the paper describes the methods and their implementation, reports benchmarks and a design-based simulation, and illustrates a complete workflow.

2 Methods

Throughout, areas are indexed by \(d = 1, \dots, D\), \(\boldsymbol{x}_d\) is a \(p\)-vector of area-level covariates, and \(\psi_d\) is the design-based sampling variance of the direct estimate \(y_d\) of the area parameter \(\theta_d\).

2.1 Frequentist models

The Fay-Herriot model (Fay and Herriot 1979) is \[\begin{equation} y_d = \boldsymbol{x}_d^\top \boldsymbol{\beta} + u_d + e_d, \quad u_d \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2), \quad e_d \overset{\mathrm{ind}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \psi_d), \tag{1} \end{equation}\] with \(\psi_d\) known. Its empirical best linear unbiased predictor (EBLUP) is \(\hat{\theta}_d = \hat{\gamma}_d y_d + (1 - \hat{\gamma}_d)\boldsymbol{x}_d^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) with \(\hat{\gamma}_d = \hat{\sigma}_u^2 / (\hat{\sigma}_u^2 + \psi_d)\), where \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is the generalized least squares estimator at the REML or ML estimate \(\hat{\sigma}_u^2\). The MSE is estimated by the second-order approximation \(g_{1d} + g_{2d} + 2g_{3d}\) of Prasad and Rao (1990) for REML, with the bias correction of Datta and Lahiri (2000) for ML, or by parametric bootstrap.

The spatial Fay-Herriot model replaces the independent area effects by a simultaneous autoregressive (SAR) process, \(\boldsymbol{u} = \rho\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{u} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) with \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma_u^2\boldsymbol{I})\) and a row-standardized proximity matrix \(\boldsymbol{W}\) (Petrucci and Salvati 2006; Pratesi and Salvati 2008). The marginal covariance is then \(\boldsymbol{V} = \boldsymbol{G} + \boldsymbol{\Psi}\) with \(\boldsymbol{G} = \sigma_u^2[(\boldsymbol{I} - \rho\boldsymbol{W})^\top(\boldsymbol{I} - \rho\boldsymbol{W})]^{-1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Psi} = \mathrm{diag}(\psi_d)\), and the EBLUP is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \boldsymbol{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{G}}\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\). The process is defined on all areas of \(\boldsymbol{W}\), so areas without a sample are predicted as well. \((\sigma_u^2, \rho)\) are estimated by REML or ML Fisher scoring on \(|\rho| \le 0.999\), with a projected iteration when the unconstrained one ends at a negative variance. MSEs follow Molina and Marhuenda (2015) (analytical, parametric or non-parametric bootstrap).

For repeated surveys, \(t = 1, \dots, T\), the spatio-temporal model of Marhuenda et al. (2013) is \[\begin{equation} y_{dt} = \boldsymbol{x}_{dt}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} + u_{1d} + u_{2dt} + e_{dt}, \tag{2} \end{equation}\] with a SAR area effect \(u_{1d}\) and an AR(1) effect \(u_{2dt} = \rho_2 u_{2d,t-1} + \varepsilon_{2dt}\) within each area. The panel need not be complete: the model is defined on the full \(D \times T\) grid, the variance parameters are estimated by REML from the observed cells, and missing periods and areas that were never sampled are predicted from the observed ones (Saei and Chambers 2005), with analytical and parametric bootstrap MSEs for every cell.

At the unit level, the nested error model of Battese et al. (1988), \(y_{dj} = \boldsymbol{x}_{dj}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} + u_d + e_{dj}\) for unit \(j\) of area \(d\), gives the EBLUP of the area mean \(\hat{\bar{Y}}_d = f_d\bar{y}_d + (1 - f_d)\{\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{d,r}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} + \hat{\gamma}_d(\bar{y}_d - \bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_d^\top\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\}\), where \(f_d = n_d/N_d\) is the sampling fraction and \(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{d,r}\) the covariate mean of the non-sampled units. eblup_twofold() fits the two-fold model with area and sub-area effects in the same way.

2.2 Hierarchical Bayes models

Latent Gaussian models

The HB functions formulate each model as a latent Gaussian model (Rue et al. 2009). For hb_area(), \[\begin{equation} y_d \mid \mu_d \sim \pi(y_d \mid \mu_d, \phi_d), \qquad g(\mu_d) = \boldsymbol{x}_d^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} + u_d, \tag{3} \end{equation}\] where \(g\) is the identity, logit or log link of the family, \(\phi_d\) a dispersion parameter (below), and \(\boldsymbol{u}\) a zero-mean Gaussian Markov random field: independent effects or one of the spatial structures below, plus temporal and space-time terms for panel data. hb_unit() applies the same construction to unit records with an area effect, and hb_twofold() to sub-areas within areas. The INLA engine approximates the posterior marginals by nested Laplace approximations and numerical integration over the hyperparameters; fastsae uses the full Laplace approximation by default, which is more accurate than the simplified one for non-Gaussian likelihoods with little information per area. The JAX engine samples the same model with the No-U-Turn sampler.

Dispersion from the sampling variances

When the sampling variances \(\psi_d\) are known, fastsae fixes the dispersion of each area so that the model variance of \(y_d\) equals \(\psi_d\):

  • Gaussian: the observation variance is \(\psi_d\), which gives the Fay-Herriot model (1).
  • Beta: for proportions with a logit link, \(\mathrm{Var}(y_d) = \mu_d(1 - \mu_d)/(1 + \phi_d)\), which gives the precision of Janicki (2020), \[\begin{equation} \phi_d = \max\left\{\frac{\mu_d(1 - \mu_d)}{\psi_d} - 1, \, 1\right\}. \tag{4} \end{equation}\] It depends on the unknown \(\mu_d\). Replacing \(\mu_d\) by \(y_d\) makes the model variance \(\psi_d\mu_d(1 - \mu_d)/\{y_d(1 - y_d)\}\), too small wherever \(y_d\) is far from \(\mu_d\); in a simulation with proportions near 0.08 the 95% intervals then covered 81–84% of the true values. fastsae therefore fits the model twice and evaluates (4) at the posterior means of the first fit, which gave 90–94%. tipsae instead makes \(\phi_d\) a function of \(\mu_d\) inside the model (De Nicolò and Gardini 2024); this covered 93–95% in the same simulation, but makes the likelihood bimodal, with a mode of low precision near zero, and 11 of 240 fits did not converge (\(\hat{R} > 1.05\)). With known sample sizes instead of variances, \(\phi_d = n_d - 1\).
  • Gamma: for positive skewed indicators with a log link, \(\mathrm{Var}(y_d) = \mu_d^2/s_d\) gives the shape \(s_d = y_d^2/\psi_d\), the inverse squared coefficient of variation.
  • Negative binomial: \(\mathrm{Var}(y_d) = \mu_d + \mu_d^2/k_d\) gives the size \(k_d = \hat{\mu}_d^2/(\psi_d - \hat{\mu}_d)\), again at the posterior means of a first fit.
  • Binomial and Poisson: sample sizes are the numbers of trials, and population exposures enter as an offset.

Without sampling variances, one dispersion parameter is estimated. With one direct estimate per area it trades off against the area effect, so its prior determines the intervals (Table 2), and hb_area() warns.

Spatial and space-time effects

The area effect supports the intrinsic conditional autoregressive (ICAR) model of Besag et al. (1991), the BYM model with independent and ICAR components, the scaled BYM2 model \(u_d = \sigma_u(\sqrt{1 - \phi}\,v_d + \sqrt{\phi}\,u^*_d)\) of Riebler et al. (2016), where \(u^*\) is a scaled ICAR field, the Leroux model with precision \(\tau[(1 - \lambda)\boldsymbol{I} + \lambda(\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{1}) - \boldsymbol{W})]\) (Leroux et al. 2000), and SAR models for the area effect, as in the spatial Fay-Herriot model, or as a spatial lag model. Temporal effects can be random walks of order one or two, AR(1) or independent, and space-time interactions can be area-specific trends, a spatial field with AR(1) dynamics, or one of the four types of Knorr-Held (2000).

With few areas, or one observation per area, the data say little about the variance and dispersion parameters, and their priors decide how strongly the estimates are shrunk and how wide the intervals are. Table 2 lists the defaults, which both engines share.

The half-normal prior is a weakly informative choice for hierarchical standard deviations (Gelman 2006). Its scale is tied to the response, because a penalized complexity (PC) prior \(P(\sigma > U) = \alpha\) (Simpson et al. 2017) with the common \(U = 1\) depends on the unit of \(y\): in a Fay-Herriot simulation with 30 areas the 95% intervals under PC\((1, 0.01)\) covered 94%, 85% and 38% of the true means for the same data in three units. The scaled half-normal prior covered as well as the EBLUP intervals (93–97% for 15 to 60 areas, against 93–96%), whereas PC priors with \(U = \mathrm{sd}(\boldsymbol{y})\) covered 85–94%, because they put much mass near \(\sigma = 0\) (Sørbye and Rue 2014). On the logit and log scales, half-normal\((1)\) covered at least as well as the half-normal\((2.5)\) default of tipsae and estimated the area standard deviation with less bias. For the dispersion parameters estimated without \(\psi_d\), the INLA defaults put the gamma shape near 80 and the negative binomial size near 110–180 whatever the truth, and intervals for overdispersed data covered 52–92% of the true means; the half-normal\((10)\) prior covered 90–99%, at the cost of too wide intervals when the sampling noise is small, which is why \(\psi_d\) should be supplied whenever it is known. These simulations and their code are documented in vignette("hb-priors").

2.3 Benchmarking

Small area estimates \(\hat{\theta}_d\) are often required to agree with direct estimates of larger domains (Rao and Molina 2015; Stefan and Hidiroglou 2021). Let areas \(d\) belong to groups \(g\) (provinces, say) with weights \(w_d\) (population shares) normalized within groups, \(\tilde{w}_d = w_d / \sum_{d' \in g} w_{d'}\), and let \(T_g\) be the group targets. benchmark_sae() adjusts the estimates so that \(\sum_{d \in g}\tilde{w}_d\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{B}}_d = T_g\) by

  • ratio adjustment, \(\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{B}}_d = \hat{\theta}_d T_g / \sum_{d' \in g}\tilde{w}_{d'}\hat{\theta}_{d'}\) (You and Rao 2002);
  • difference adjustment, \(\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{B}}_d = \hat{\theta}_d + T_g - \sum_{d' \in g}\tilde{w}_{d'}\hat{\theta}_{d'}\);
  • MSE-weighted adjustment, which minimizes \(\sum_d(\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{B}}_d - \hat{\theta}_d)^2/\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_d)\) under the constraint and so moves imprecise estimates most (Datta et al. 2011; Bell et al. 2013): \[\begin{equation} \hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{B}}_d = \hat{\theta}_d + \frac{\tilde{w}_d\,\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_d)}{\sum_{d' \in g}\tilde{w}_{d'}^2\,\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_{d'})}\Big(T_g - \sum_{d' \in g}\tilde{w}_{d'}\hat{\theta}_{d'}\Big); \tag{5} \end{equation}\]
  • a shift \(\alpha_g\) on the logit scale, \(\sum_{d \in g}\tilde{w}_d\,\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\{\mathrm{logit}(\hat{\theta}_d) + \alpha_g\} = T_g\), solved by one-dimensional root finding, which keeps proportions inside \((0, 1)\).

With a national target \(T\) as well, the group targets are first adjusted so that their weighted mean equals \(T\), and the areas are then benchmarked to the adjusted group targets. hb_area() and eblup_fh() can also benchmark within the fit (argument self_benchmark); for hb_area(), the posterior draws are then benchmarked, so the intervals refer to the benchmarked estimates.

2.4 Reliability classification

Statistical offices publish small area estimates by tiers of their relative standard error, \(\mathrm{RSE}_d = \sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_d)}/|\hat{\theta}_d|\). add_reliability_flags() labels an estimate reliable for \(\mathrm{RSE}_d \le c_1\), to be used with caution for \(c_1 < \mathrm{RSE}_d \le c_2\), and unreliable above \(c_2\). The defaults \(c_1 = 25\%\) and \(c_2 = 50\%\) are those of BPS-Statistics Indonesia; other cut-offs can be supplied.

3 Implementation

Table 3 lists the main functions. The frequentist functions call C++ code (RcppArmadillo, RcppEigen) that never forms a dense inverse of the marginal covariance matrix. The spatial model works with sparse Cholesky factors of \((\boldsymbol{I} - \rho\boldsymbol{W})^\top(\boldsymbol{I} - \rho\boldsymbol{W})\) and of \((\boldsymbol{I} - \rho\boldsymbol{W})^\top(\boldsymbol{I} - \rho\boldsymbol{W}) + \sigma_u^2\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\) through the Woodbury identity; the spatio-temporal and two-fold models compute REML scores, Fisher information and the analytical MSE from \(T \times T\), \(D \times D\) and low-rank blocks; and the unit-level model works with area-level sufficient statistics, profiling \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) and \(\sigma_e^2\) out of the (restricted) likelihood. Bootstrap replicates run in parallel with OpenMP; their random numbers are drawn beforehand from R’s generator, so the results depend on the seed but not on the number of threads.

The HB functions translate formulas, adjacency information and sampling variances into a model and pass it to an engine object. inla_engine() (the default) holds the INLA strategy, the priors and further arguments of INLA::inla(); jax_engine() holds the device (CPU or GPU), the number of chains and draws and the sampler settings, and runs NumPyro through reticulate. Both engines return the same object, so print(), summary(), plot(), coef(), fitted() and the post-estimation functions do not depend on how a model was estimated. INLA is not on CRAN and Python is optional, so both are suggested dependencies, as are sf for maps and a spreadsheet writer for export; the frequentist and post-estimation functions need neither INLA nor Python.

Its time grows faster with \(D\) than that of INLA, because it represents the ICAR effect through the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian, which costs \(O(D^2)\) per gradient, and for the beta model it runs the sampler twice, once for each stage of the precision. On the CPU it took 0.4 times the time of tipsae at \(D = 100\) and 2.0 times at \(D = 2,500\). The Apple GPU has a fixed cost of about 2 minutes per sampler run, as each step of the tree-building loop of NUTS synchronizes GPU and CPU; it pays off only for the largest models. Running NumPyro on Apple GPUs required replacing two operations that jax-metal cannot compile (popcnt and lax.cond with constant outputs). The posterior means of JAX correlated with those of INLA at \(r \ge 0.9999\) with median ratios of posterior standard deviations between 1.00 and 1.02. The largest split \(\hat{R}\) of the JAX fits was 1.016, slightly above the threshold of 1.01 recommended by Vehtari et al. (2021), so longer chains than these two of 1,000 draws are advisable for inference. Not all tipsae fits converged: the largest \(\hat{R}\) was 3.71, at \(D = 100\), in line with the bimodal likelihood described under Dispersion from the sampling variances, so its times refer to the run length and not to a converged posterior.

4.4 Design-based simulation

To assess the estimators under repeated sampling from a finite population, we generated a population of about \(N = 250{,}000\) individuals in \(D = 500\) areas on a \(20 \times 25\) grid, with a binary outcome that is logistic in two covariates with ICAR and independent area effects, and drew \(R = 25\) samples of 20 to 50 units per area by simple random sampling. Each sample was fitted by a beta model with an ICAR area effect and the sampling variances of the direct proportions, by hb_area() with its defaults and by tipsae (type_disp = "var", one chain of 500 iterations after 150 warm-up iterations). The script is benchmarks/sim_mc_finite_pop_d500.R.

6 Summary and discussion

fastsae brings the frequentist and hierarchical Bayes small area models used in official statistics, and the steps that follow a fit, together in one interface. The compiled frequentist implementations reduce the time of the spatio-temporal Fay-Herriot model with analytical MSE at 1,000 areas from half an hour to about a minute, and its memory allocated by R from gigabytes to megabytes. The HB functions fit beta and other non-Gaussian models in seconds with INLA, and the same models can be checked against full MCMC with the JAX engine without rewriting them.

Some limitations should be kept in mind. INLA has a fixed cost per fit, so for small Gaussian models numerical integration as in hbsae is faster: for the Fay-Herriot model with 250 areas, hb_area() took 0.70 s and hbsae::fSAE.Area() 0.05 s (script benchmarks/bench_hb_normal.R). The beta and negative binomial dispersions are fixed at first-stage posterior means, and their uncertainty is not propagated. INLA is an approximation whose accuracy can deteriorate for non-Gaussian likelihoods with very little information per area; the JAX engine allows such cases to be checked. Our benchmarks were run on a single machine, and the runtimes of sae and emdi were not measured on the same draws as those of fastsae.

Future work will add unit-level models for non-Gaussian outcomes, zero-inflated beta models for indicators with many zeros, and benchmarking that propagates into the MSE of frequentist estimates.

Computational details

The results were obtained with R 4.6.1, fastsae 1.0.0, R-INLA 26.8.7, tipsae 1.0.3, sae 1.3, emdi 2.2 and hbsae 1.2; the JAX engine used JAX 0.5.0 and NumPyro 0.19.0. R and the packages are available from CRAN at https://CRAN.R-project.org/, R-INLA from https://www.r-inla.org/, and the source of fastsae, including the benchmark scripts, from https://github.com/ridsonap/fastsae.
