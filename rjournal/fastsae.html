Fastsae: High-Performance Frequentist and Bayesian Small Area Estimation in R

Small area estimation (SAE) plays a pivotal operational role in official statistics, enabling the production of reliable disaggregated socioeconomic, health, and demographic indicators when domain-specific sample sizes are small or zero. However, practitioners in national statistical offices (NSOs) face a triple computational and operational barrier: frequentist spatial and spatio-temporal models with bootstrap mean squared error (MSE) estimation suffer from severe memory bottlenecks and slow runtimes in interpreted languages; non-Gaussian indicators (such as rates, skewed expenditures, and counts) traditionally require computationally demanding Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms that impede routine production; and model estimates often fail to aggregate to published higher-level totals, violating institutional consistency. We introduce fastsae, an R package engineered for high-performance frequentist and Bayesian small area estimation. On the frequentist side, fastsae implements optimized C++17 routines via RcppArmadillo paired with OpenMP multi-threading, accelerating the classical Fay-Herriot, spatial Fay-Herriot, spatio-temporal Fay-Herriot, and unit-level Battese-Harter-Fuller models alongside their analytical and bootstrap MSE estimators. On the Bayesian side, fastsae provides a unified hierarchical Bayes (HB) interface across six distribution families (Gaussian, Beta, Gamma, Binomial, Poisson, Negative Binomial), fitted by Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (INLA) by default or by MCMC in Python (JAX/NumPyro) through interchangeable engine objects. The likelihood dispersion of each area is fixed at its design-based sampling variance, the remaining hyperparameters have documented default priors that were calibrated by simulation, and the models accommodate modern spatial priors (BYM, scaled BYM2, Leroux CAR, and SLM) as well as Knorr-Held space-time interaction dynamics. To bridge estimation and dissemination, the package features a two-stage hierarchical benchmarking engine for simultaneous provincial and national calibration, an automated reliability classification engine aligned with NSO standards, multi-model concordances, automated ggplot2 spatial mapping, and multi-sheet spreadsheet exports. Extensive benchmarks and finite population Monte Carlo simulations demonstrate large computational speedups at 1,000 domains (1,263x for Fay-Herriot and 67x for spatial Fay-Herriot relative to sae, 217x for spatial Beta relative to Stan-based MCMC) and reductions of more than 99% in allocated memory, without loss of inferential precision.

In modern evidence-based governance, policy interventions are increasingly decentralized. Monitoring the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), allocating municipal fiscal transfers, targeting social safety net subsidies, and responding to localized disease outbreaks all require dependable statistical indicators at highly disaggregated geographic administrative levels . However, national statistical offices (NSOs) face structural sample size limitations. Large-scale household surveys—such as the American Community Survey (ACS) in the United States, the European Union Statistics on Income and Living Conditions (EU-SILC), and the National Socioeconomic Survey (Susenas) in Indonesia—are powered to yield acceptable sampling errors only at national or broad regional levels. When partitioned into sub-domains (e.g., municipalities, districts, or health zones), domain-specific sample sizes become sparse or zero, causing direct survey estimators to suffer from high sampling variance and unacceptable coefficients of variation .

Small area estimation (SAE) overcomes these limitations by ``borrowing strength’’ across domains and time periods via linking models that combine survey responses with auxiliary administrative registers, census aggregates, or geospatial layers . The cornerstone of this literature remains the area-level Fay-Herriot (FH) model , extended to account for spatial dependencies via Simultaneous Autoregressive (SAR) structures and temporal dynamics via panel autoregressive processes . In the presence of micro-level auxiliary data, the nested error regression model of (BHF) provides unit-level empirical best linear unbiased predictors (EBLUP).

Despite these theoretical advances, operationalizing small area models within production environments in NSOs has been hindered by three structural bottlenecks: %

As summarized in Table \(\ref{tab:pkg_comparison}\), the current software landscape is compartmentalized. Foundational toolkits such as sae are implemented in interpreted R, lack multi-threading, and are restricted to Gaussian linear frameworks. Packages such as emdi offer visualizations but lack spatio-temporal dynamics and non-Gaussian spatial Bayesian likelihoods. Specialized packages such as tipsae implement Beta regression but depend on MCMC runtimes that scale poorly for panel graphs. Toolkits such as mcmcsae remain simulation-based, while SUMMER is tailored to demographic survey mortality rather than general survey design-variance matching.

To resolve these limitations, this paper introduces fastsae. Specifically, this paper makes four concrete, testable contributions: %

Let \(m\) denote the number of small areas indexed by \(i = 1, \dots, m\). The classical area-level model is defined by: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_model} y_i = \bm{x}_i^\top \bm{\beta} + u_i + e_i, \quad u_i \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2), \quad e_i \overset{\mathrm{ind}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, D_i), \end{equation}\] % where \(y_i\) is the design-based direct estimator of the target indicator \(\theta_i\), \(\bm{x}_i\) is a \(p \times 1\) vector of auxiliary covariates, \(D_i\) is the known design variance, and \(u_i\) is the area random effect. The Empirical Best Linear Unbiased Predictor (EBLUP) is given by: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_eblup} \hat{\theta}_i = \hat{\gamma}_i y_i + (1 - \hat{\gamma}_i) \bm{x}_i^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}}, \quad \hat{\gamma}_i = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_u^2}{\hat{\sigma}_u^2 + D_i}, \end{equation}\] % where \(\hat{\bm{\beta}} = (\bm{X}^\top \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} \bm{X})^{-1} \bm{X}^\top \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} \bm{y}\) with \(\bm{V} = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_u^2 + D_1, \dots, \sigma_u^2 + D_m)\). Mean squared error is evaluated via the second-order Prasad-Rao approximation : % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_mse} \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_i) \approx g_{1i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2) + g_{2i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2) + 2 g_{3i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2). \end{equation}\]

To accommodate spatial dependence across contiguous domains, and modeled the area random effects \(\bm{u} = (u_1, \dots, u_m)^\top\) via a Simultaneous Autoregressive (SAR) process: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:sfh_sar} \bm{u} = \rho \bm{W} \bm{u} + \bm{\epsilon}, \quad \bm{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}_m(\bm{0}, \sigma_1^2 \bm{I}_m), \end{equation}\] % where \(\rho\) is the spatial autoregressive parameter and \(\bm{W}\) is a row-standardized spatial proximity matrix. The marginal covariance matrix is \(\bm{V}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) = \bm{G}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) + \bm{D}\), where \(\bm{G}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) = \sigma_1^2 [(\bm{I}_m - \rho \bm{W})^\top (\bm{I}_m - \rho \bm{W})]^{-1}\). The spatial EBLUP is: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:sfh_eblup} \hat{\bm{\theta}} = \bm{X} \hat{\bm{\beta}} + \hat{\bm{G}} \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} (\bm{y} - \bm{X} \hat{\bm{\beta}}). \end{equation}\] % The SAR process is defined on all domains of \(\bm{W}\), including unsampled ones, which are predicted by spatial kriging. Variance components \((\sigma_1^2, \rho)\) are estimated by REML or ML Fisher scoring with \(|\rho| \le 0.999\), falling back to a constrained (projected) Fisher scoring when the classical iteration ends at a negative variance. All quantities are obtained from sparse Cholesky factorizations of \(\bm{A} = (\bm{I} - \rho\bm{W})^\top(\bm{I} - \rho\bm{W})\) and \(\bm{A} + \sigma_1^2 \bm{E}^\top \bm{D}^{-1} \bm{E}\) through the Woodbury identity, so no dense \(m \times m\) inverse is formed. Mean squared error uses the same second-order approximation \(g_1 + g_2 + 2g_3 - g_4\) as sae , or parametric and non-parametric bootstrap.

When small area data are collected across longitudinal waves \(t = 1, \dots, T\), borrowing strength across space and time yields substantial efficiency gains : % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:stfh_model} y_{it} = \bm{x}_{it}^\top \bm{\beta} + u_{1,it} + u_{2,it} + e_{it}, \end{equation}\] % where \(\bm{u}_1 = \rho_1 \bm{W} \bm{u}_1 + \bm{\epsilon}_1\) is a spatial SAR effect that is constant over time within each area (\(u_{1,it} = u_{1,i}\)) and \(u_{2,it} = \rho_2 u_{2,i,t-1} + \epsilon_{2,it}\) follows an AR(1) temporal process with \(|\rho_2| < 1\), as in . In fastsae, the covariance \(\bm{V} = \bm{A} + \bm{Z}\bm{V}_{u_1}\bm{Z}^\top\) (with \(\bm{A}\) block diagonal in \(T \times T\) blocks) is never formed: REML scores, Fisher information, and the analytical MSE (\(g_1 + g_2 + 2g_3 - g_4\)) are computed from \(T \times T\), \(D \times D\), and low-rank \(DT \times (D + p)\) pieces via the Woodbury identity. The panel need not be complete: the model is defined on the full \(D \times T\) grid, the variance components are estimated by REML from the sampled cells, and missing periods as well as areas that are never sampled are predicted by the best linear unbiased predictor given the sampled data , with analytical (second-order) and parametric bootstrap MSE for every cell.

When unit microdata are accessible alongside population auxiliary totals, fastsae implements the nested error regression model : % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:bhf_model} y_{dj} = \bm{x}_{dj}^\top \bm{\beta} + u_d + e_{dj}, \quad d = 1, \dots, D, \quad j = 1, \dots, n_d, \end{equation}\] % yielding EBLUP predictors \(\hat{\bar{Y}}_d = f_d \bar{y}_d + (1 - f_d)(\bar{\bm{X}}_{d, r}^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}} + \hat{\gamma}_d (\bar{y}_d - \bar{\bm{x}}_d^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}}))\), where \(f_d = n_d / N_d\).

The Bayesian functions formulate small area estimation as a latent Gaussian model (LGM) . For the area-level model of , % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:lgm_formulation} y_i \mid \mu_i, \psi_i \sim \pi(y_i \mid \mu_i, \psi_i), \quad \eta_i = g(\mu_i) = \bm{x}_i^\top \bm{\beta} + u_i, \end{equation}\] % where \(g(\cdot)\) is the canonical link of the family (identity, logit or log), \(\psi_i\) a dispersion parameter (described below) and \(\bm{u}\) a zero-mean Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF): independent effects \(u_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2)\) or one of the spatial structures below, with temporal and space-time terms added for panel data. applies the same construction to unit records with a domain effect, and to sub-areas nested in areas, with an area effect \(v_i\) and an independent sub-area effect \(u_{ij}\). INLA approximates the posterior marginals of the area parameters \(\mu_i\) by nested Laplace approximations and numerical integration over the hyperparameters; fastsae uses the full Laplace approximation () by default, which is more accurate than the simplified Laplace approximation for non-Gaussian likelihoods with little information per area.

To preserve the design-based sampling variances \(D_i\) of the direct estimates, fastsae fixes the dispersion parameter of each area at the value for which the model variance equals \(D_i\): %

% Without sampling variances, a single dispersion parameter is estimated. With one direct estimate per area it trades off against the area effect, and its prior then determines the intervals (Table \(\ref{tab:priors}\)); warns in this case.

The area effect \(\bm{u}\) supports five graph-based structures: the intrinsic CAR (ICAR) model of , the BYM model with independent and ICAR components, the scaled BYM2 model of , \(u_i = \sigma_u(\sqrt{1 - \phi}\, v_i + \sqrt{\phi}\, u^*_i)\) with \(u^*\) a scaled ICAR field, the Leroux CAR with precision \(\bm{Q} = \tau[(1 - \lambda)\bm{I} + \lambda(\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}) - \bm{W})]\) , and simultaneous autoregressive models, either for the area effect, \(\bm{u} = (\bm{I} - \rho\bm{W})^{-1}\bm{\varepsilon}\) as in the spatial Fay-Herriot model, or as a spatial lag model (SLM). For panel observations across \(T \ge 2\), temporal trends support random walks (RW1, RW2), AR(1) and independent effects. Space-time interactions can be specified as domain-specific trends, a separable spatial field with AR(1) dynamics, or according to the four interaction types (\(\bm{Q}_{st} = \bm{Q}_s \otimes \bm{Q}_t\) for type IV).

With few areas, or one observation per area, the data hardly inform the variance and dispersion parameters, and their priors decide how strongly the estimates are shrunk and how wide the intervals are. Table \(\ref{tab:priors}\) lists the defaults, which are shared by the INLA and JAX engines so that both fit the same model.

The half-normal prior is the weakly informative choice of for hierarchical standard deviations. Its scale is tied to the response: a penalized complexity (PC) prior \(P(\sigma > U) = \alpha\) is a good default when \(U\) is chosen for the problem, but the common \(U = 1\), \(\alpha = 0.01\) depends on the unit of \(y\). In a Fay-Herriot simulation with 30 areas the 95% intervals under PC\((1, 0.01)\) covered 94%, 85% and 38% of the true means for the same data expressed in three units, while the scaled half-normal prior covered as the EBLUP intervals (93–97% for 15 to 60 areas, EBLUP 93–96%), and PC priors with \(U = \mathrm{sd}(\bm{y})\) still covered only 85–94% because they put much mass near \(\sigma = 0\). On the logit and log scales, half-normal\((1)\) covered at least as well as half-normal\((2.5)\), the default of tipsae, and estimated the area SD with less bias. For the dispersion parameters estimated without \(D_i\), INLA’s own defaults put the Gamma shape near 80 and the Negative Binomial size near 110–180 whatever the truth, and intervals for overdispersed data then covered 52–92% of the true means; the half-normal\((10)\) prior covered 90–99%, at the cost of too wide intervals when the sampling noise is small, which is why \(D_i\) should be supplied whenever it is known. The simulations and the code behind them are documented in the package vignette .

In official statistics, model-based small area estimates \(\hat{\theta}_{di}\) for domain \(i\) in province/region \(g\) must aggregate to officially published higher-level targets . Let \(w_{di}\) denote domain population weights such that \(W_g = \sum_{i \in g} w_{di}\) is the provincial total weight, and \(W_{\text{nat}} = \sum_g W_g\) is the national total weight. Let \(T_g\) denote the provincial target and \(T_{\text{nat}}\) denote the national aggregate target.

fastsae implements a two-stage hierarchical benchmarking calibration: % % Across both stages, fastsae supports four calibration mechanisms: %

NSOs classify domain estimates into publication tiers based on the Relative Standard Error (\(\mathrm{RSE} = \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_i)}}{|\hat{\theta}_i|} \times 100\%\)). fastsae provides implementing institutional thresholding: % \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:reliability} \mathrm{Tier}_i = \begin{cases} \text{Reliable (Direct Publish)}, & \mathrm{RSE}_i \le c_1, \\ \text{Use with Caution (Accompanying Warning)}, & c_1 < \mathrm{RSE}_i \le c_2, \\ \text{Unreliable (Suppress or Aggregate)}, & \mathrm{RSE}_i > c_2, \end{cases} \end{equation}\] % where the default thresholds \((c_1, c_2) = (25\%, 50\%)\) follow the criteria of BPS-Statistics Indonesia (with \(\mathrm{RSE} \le c_1\) reliable and \(\mathrm{RSE} > c_2\) unreliable); other agencies’ cut-offs can be supplied.

The architecture of fastsae unites compiled linear algebra with high-level Bayesian GMRF modeling and production reporting. Figure \(\ref{fig:arch}\) outlines the structural flow.

The frequentist engines (, , , , ) never form a dense inverse of the marginal covariance: the spatial model works with sparse Cholesky factors (RcppEigen), the spatio-temporal and two-fold models with block-structured Woodbury identities, and the unit-level model with domain-level sufficient statistics, profiling \(\bm{\beta}\) and \(\sigma_e^2\) out of the (restricted) likelihood. The bootstrap MSE loops run in parallel with OpenMP (); random numbers are drawn sequentially from R’s generator beforehand, so results depend only on the seed and not on the number of threads. The Bayesian functions (, , ) translate formulas, spatial adjacency matrices \(\bm{W}\), and survey dispersion arguments into R-INLA calls. How a model is estimated is specified separately from the model by an engine object: holds the INLA strategy, priors, and further arguments, and fits models by NUTS sampling in JAX/NumPyro (CPU or GPU) through reticulate. Standard S3 methods (, , , , , ) are implemented across all model classes.

We evaluated computational speed and memory allocation across domain sizes \(D = 30, 50, 100, 250, 500\), and \(1000\) using simulated datasets with 5 auxiliary covariates. Times are medians measured with the bench package; memory is the total memory allocated by R during one call (), not the peak resident memory. The fastsae rows were measured with fastsae 1.0.0 under R 4.6.1 on an Apple M4 processor (10 cores); the sae and emdi rows come from an earlier run of the same design on different random draws. The scripts are in the directory of the package repository.

Table \(\ref{tab:bench_freq}\) demonstrates the scalability gains of fastsae. At \(D=1,000\), classical FH completes in about one millisecond in fastsae compared to 1.5 seconds in sae (\(1,263\times\) slower) and 51 seconds in emdi (\(42,874\times\) slower). For spatio-temporal models at \(D=1,000\) (\(T=5\)), sae required nearly 30 minutes (1,786 seconds) and allocated 32.5 gigabytes, whereas fastsae finished in 75 seconds allocating 3.3 megabytes. : fastsae eliminates out-of-memory bottlenecks, enabling routine spatio-temporal estimation across thousands of domains on standard laptop hardware.

For bounded rates, we benchmarked against tipsae using Stan (4 chains, 2,000 iterations).

As shown in Table \(\ref{tab:bench_beta}\), deterministic Laplace approximations in fastsae scale flatly with domain count: fitting a spatial Beta model at \(D=1,000\) requires only 1.87 seconds in fastsae compared to 404 seconds in tipsae (\(217\times\) speedup). Most of the fastsae time is the fixed start-up cost of the INLA program (about 1.2 seconds even at \(D = 30\)). In spatio-temporal panel models (\(D=50, T=5\)), tipsae required 15.1 minutes (908 seconds), whereas fastsae completed in 1.71 seconds (\(530\times\) speedup). Despite this speed differential, inferential concordance on \(D=1,000\) yields Pearson correlation \(r = 0.9993\), Spearman \(\rho = 0.9992\), and Mean Absolute Difference \(\mathrm{MAD} = 0.0197\). : Deterministic Laplace approximation in fastsae achieves inferential equivalence with fully converged Hamiltonian MCMC in seconds rather than minutes or hours.

The same model can be estimated by INLA () or by full MCMC with the No-U-Turn sampler of NumPyro on JAX (), on the CPU or on a GPU. We compared the engines on spatial models with a Besag (ICAR) area effect on a regular grid with queen adjacency, for a Gaussian Fay-Herriot model and a Beta model; for the Beta model, tipsae was fitted with its ICAR spatial error. Each engine ran in its own R process on an Apple M4 (10 cores, 16 GB), JAX with 2 chains of 1,000 draws after 500 warmup, and each fit was run twice on the same data; Table \(\ref{tab:bench_engines}\) reports the second (warm) run.

INLA is the fastest engine at every size: at \(D = 2,500\) it fits the spatial Beta model in 3.2 s, 296 times faster than tipsae. Among the MCMC options, JAX on the CPU is 2 to 18 times faster than Stan, with the gap narrowing as \(D\) grows, because the JAX engine represents the ICAR effect through the eigenvectors of the \(D \times D\) graph Laplacian (\(O(D^2)\) work per gradient). The Apple GPU carries a fixed cost of about two minutes per fit, since each step of the NUTS tree-building loop synchronises the GPU and the CPU, but it overtakes the CPU at \(D = 2,500\) for both models. Running NumPyro’s NUTS on Apple GPUs required two workarounds in fastsae, because jax-metal cannot compile the bit count or statements with constant outputs; both are replaced by equivalent operations when . The engines agree closely: the posterior means of JAX on either device correlate with INLA at \(r \geq 0.9999\), and the largest split \(\hat{R}\) over all JAX fits is 1.007 (1.083 for tipsae with its default run length).

To assess frequentist design properties, we conducted design-based Monte Carlo simulations under known ground truth at national scale (\(D = 500\) small areas on a \(20 \times 25\) grid, \(R = 25\) independent sample draws) using a synthetic finite population of \(N = 250,000\) individuals with a binary outcome, logistic in two covariates with spatial (ICAR) and independent area effects. Each replicate draws 20 to 50 units per area by simple random sampling and fits a Beta model with an ICAR area effect and the sampling variances of the direct proportions, by with its default priors and by tipsae (, one chain of 500 iterations after 150 warmup). The script is in the package repository.

Table \(\ref{tab:mc_truth}\) shows that reduces the empirical RMSE from 0.0719 (direct survey estimator) to 0.0489, an empirical efficiency gain (ratio of mean squared errors) of \(1.99\), and the relative RMSE from \(27.1\%\) to \(18.9\%\). The two Bayesian fits are practically equivalent in accuracy (RMSE 0.0489 against 0.0487) and in the coverage of their 95% credible intervals (93.4% and 93.7%), with slightly narrower intervals for fastsae, which needed about a fifth of the runtime per fit. An earlier version of this simulation used a PC prior on the area SD and the plug-in Beta precision \(y_i(1 - y_i)/D_i - 1\); its intervals covered only 89.0% of the true proportions, against 93.4% with the two-stage precision and the default priors of Table \(\ref{tab:priors}\).

We demonstrate an end-to-end NSO production workflow using the benchmark dataset (\(D = 42\) domains across 6 provincial groups).

library("fastsae")
data("sim_area")
sim_area$prov <- rep(paste0("Prov_", 1:6), each = 7)
sim_area$pop <- round(runif(nrow(sim_area), 15000, 45000))

# Construct row-standardized spatial proximity matrix
coords <- cbind(sim_area$x_coord, sim_area$y_coord)
dists <- as.matrix(dist(coords))
W <- (dists < quantile(dists, 0.18)) * 1
diag(W) <- 0
rs <- rowSums(W); rs[rs == 0] <- 1; W_std <- W / rs

# Fit Frequentist and Bayesian Models
fit_fh <- eblup_fh(y_gaussian ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  data = sim_area, method = "REML", print_result = FALSE)
fit_sfh <- eblup_sfh(y_gaussian ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  domain = "area", W = W_std, data = sim_area,
  method = "REML", print_result = FALSE)
fit_beta <- hb_area(y_beta ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  data = sim_area, family = "beta", spatial = "bym2",
  W = W, print_result = FALSE)

Small area estimation in official statistics requires reconciling mathematical rigor with strict computational and operational constraints. fastsae resolves these challenges by bridging high-performance compiled linear algebra, deterministic Bayesian approximations, and production-ready dissemination tools.

As synthesized in Table \(\ref{tab:synthesis}\), fastsae provides substantial efficiency gains across both frequentist and Bayesian paradigms. Future developments will focus on expanding unit-level spatial EBP under non-Gaussian likelihoods, integrating zero-inflated Beta (ZIB) specifications for extreme sparse indicators, and supporting direct spatial polygon streaming.

The fastsae results in this paper were obtained using R 4.6.1 with fastsae 1.0.0, R-INLA 26.8.7, and ggplot2 4.0.3 on an Apple M4 processor; the comparison runs used sae 1.3, emdi 2.2, tipsae 1.0, and hbsae 1.2.
