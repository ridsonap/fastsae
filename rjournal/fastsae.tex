\title{Fastsae: High-Performance Frequentist and Bayesian Small Area Estimation in R}


\author{by Ridson Al Farizal P. and Azka Ubaidillah}

\maketitle

\abstract{%
Small area estimation (SAE) plays a pivotal operational role in official statistics, enabling the production of reliable disaggregated socioeconomic, health, and demographic indicators when domain-specific sample sizes are small or zero. However, practitioners in national statistical offices (NSOs) face a triple computational and operational barrier: frequentist spatial and spatio-temporal models with bootstrap mean squared error (MSE) estimation suffer from severe memory bottlenecks and slow runtimes in interpreted languages; non-Gaussian indicators (such as rates, skewed expenditures, and counts) traditionally require computationally demanding Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms that impede routine production; and model estimates often fail to aggregate to published higher-level totals, violating institutional consistency. We introduce fastsae, an R package engineered for high-performance frequentist and Bayesian small area estimation. On the frequentist side, fastsae implements optimized C++17 routines via RcppArmadillo paired with OpenMP multi-threading, accelerating the classical Fay-Herriot, spatial Fay-Herriot, spatio-temporal Fay-Herriot, and unit-level Battese-Harter-Fuller models alongside their analytical and bootstrap MSE estimators. On the Bayesian side, fastsae provides a unified hierarchical Bayes (HB) interface across six distribution families (Gaussian, Beta, Gamma, Binomial, Poisson, Negative Binomial), fitted by Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (INLA) by default or by MCMC in Python (JAX/NumPyro) through interchangeable engine objects. The likelihood dispersion of each area is fixed at its design-based sampling variance, the remaining hyperparameters have documented default priors that were calibrated by simulation, and the models accommodate modern spatial priors (BYM, scaled BYM2, Leroux CAR, and SLM) as well as Knorr-Held space-time interaction dynamics. To bridge estimation and dissemination, the package features a two-stage hierarchical benchmarking engine for simultaneous provincial and national calibration, an automated reliability classification engine aligned with NSO standards, multi-model concordances, automated ggplot2 spatial mapping, and multi-sheet spreadsheet exports. Extensive benchmarks and finite population Monte Carlo simulations demonstrate large computational speedups at 1,000 domains (1,263x for Fay-Herriot and 67x for spatial Fay-Herriot relative to sae, 217x for spatial Beta relative to Stan-based MCMC) and reductions of more than 99\% in allocated memory, without loss of inferential precision.
}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

In modern evidence-based governance, policy interventions are increasingly decentralized. Monitoring the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), allocating municipal fiscal transfers, targeting social safety net subsidies, and responding to localized disease outbreaks all require dependable statistical indicators at highly disaggregated geographic administrative levels \citep{rao2015small, pfeffermann2013new}. However, national statistical offices (NSOs) face structural sample size limitations. Large-scale household surveys---such as the American Community Survey (ACS) in the United States, the European Union Statistics on Income and Living Conditions (EU-SILC), and the National Socioeconomic Survey (Susenas) in Indonesia---are powered to yield acceptable sampling errors only at national or broad regional levels. When partitioned into sub-domains (e.g., municipalities, districts, or health zones), domain-specific sample sizes become sparse or zero, causing direct survey estimators to suffer from high sampling variance and unacceptable coefficients of variation \citep{pratesi2016analysis, rao2015small}.

Small area estimation (SAE) overcomes these limitations by ``borrowing strength'\,' across domains and time periods via linking models that combine survey responses with auxiliary administrative registers, census aggregates, or geospatial layers \citep{rao2015small}. The cornerstone of this literature remains the area-level Fay-Herriot (FH) model \citep{fay1979estimates}, extended to account for spatial dependencies via Simultaneous Autoregressive (SAR) structures \citep{petrucci2006small, pratesi2008small} and temporal dynamics via panel autoregressive processes \citep{marhuenda2013small}. In the presence of micro-level auxiliary data, the nested error regression model of \citet{battese1988error} (BHF) provides unit-level empirical best linear unbiased predictors (EBLUP).

Despite these theoretical advances, operationalizing small area models within production environments in NSOs has been hindered by three structural bottlenecks:
\%

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Frequentist Computational and Memory Bottlenecks}: Existing implementations of spatial and spatio-temporal Fay-Herriot models rely on interpreted languages. Estimating model variance components via Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) requires repeated inversions of $D \times D$ or $DT \times DT$ covariance matrices. When evaluating Mean Squared Error (MSE) through parametric or non-parametric bootstrap resampling \citep{gonzalez2008bootstrap}, runtimes escalate to hours. Crucially, memory allocation for dense covariance arrays grows quadratically ($O(D^2)$ to $O(D^2 T^2)$), triggering out-of-memory (OOM) crashes on large national grids ($D \ge 500$).
  \item \textbf{Distributional Inflexibility and MCMC Friction in Bayesian SAE}: Real-world welfare indicators are rarely Gaussian. Rates and proportions (poverty headcounts, unemployment, stunting) are strictly bounded in $(0, 1)$, while expenditures exhibit severe positive skewness (Gamma), and demographic events represent discrete counts (Poisson, Negative Binomial) \citep{janicki2020properties, fabrizi2011hierarchical, boubeta2016empirical}. Under non-Gaussian likelihoods the linear EBLUP is no longer appropriate; the alternatives are the empirical best predictor (EBP) of a generalized linear mixed model \citep{boubeta2016empirical} and hierarchical Bayes (HB) estimation, which also propagates the uncertainty about the variance parameters \citep{rao2015small}. Currently, Bayesian SAE software relies on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling via \pkg{Stan} or \pkg{JAGS} \citep{denicolo2024tipsae}. MCMC sampling requires lengthy runtimes, complex convergence diagnostics ($\hat{R}$, trace plots), and often suffers from divergent transitions on complex spatial graphs \citep{simpson2017penalising}.
  \item \textbf{The Institutional Benchmarking and Dissemination Gap}: In official statistics, model-based small area estimates must numerically harmonize with published higher-level totals (e.g., provincial and national direct estimates) to preserve institutional credibility and prevent contradictory policy decisions \citep{you2002small, wang2008small, stefan2021benchmarked}. Existing packages rarely offer two-stage hierarchical benchmarking. Furthermore, software ecosystems typically end at model fitting, omitting essential dissemination steps such as automated reliability tagging based on official Relative Standard Error (RSE) thresholds, model concordance evaluation, and publication-ready spatial choropleth mapping.
\end{enumerate}

As summarized in Table \ref{tab:pkg_comparison}, the current software landscape is compartmentalized. Foundational toolkits such as \CRANpkg{sae} \citep{molina2015sae} are implemented in interpreted R, lack multi-threading, and are restricted to Gaussian linear frameworks. Packages such as \CRANpkg{emdi} \citep{kreutzmann2019emdi} offer visualizations but lack spatio-temporal dynamics and non-Gaussian spatial Bayesian likelihoods. Specialized packages such as \CRANpkg{tipsae} \citep{denicolo2024tipsae} implement Beta regression but depend on MCMC runtimes that scale poorly for panel graphs. Toolkits such as \CRANpkg{mcmcsae} \citep{boonstra2022mcmcsae} remain simulation-based, while \CRANpkg{SUMMER} \citep{li2019changes} is tailored to demographic survey mortality rather than general survey design-variance matching.

To resolve these limitations, this paper introduces \CRANpkg{fastsae}. Specifically, this paper makes four concrete, testable contributions:
\%

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Compiled C++17 Core with OpenMP Acceleration}: We engineer an optimized linear algebra core in C++17 via \CRANpkg{RcppArmadillo} that speeds up classical, spatial, and spatio-temporal Fay-Herriot estimation with analytical MSE by $1,263\times$, $67\times$, and $24\times$ relative to \CRANpkg{sae} (and the classical model by $42,874\times$ relative to \CRANpkg{emdi}) at $D=1,000$, while reducing allocated memory from gigabytes to a few megabytes. Sparse Cholesky factorizations and structured Woodbury identities avoid every dense $D \times D$ (or $DT \times DT$) inverse.
  \item \textbf{Unified Deterministic Bayesian Hierarchical Modeling via INLA with Survey Variance Matching}: We formulate a Hierarchical Bayes interface (\code{hb\_area()}) spanning six probability distributions (Gaussian, Beta, Gamma, Binomial, Poisson, Negative Binomial) powered by Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations \citep{rue2009approximate}, with an optional MCMC engine in Python (JAX/NumPyro) selected through the same interface (\code{engine = inla\_engine()} or \code{engine = jax\_engine()}). It fixes the likelihood dispersion at the survey sampling variances---the Beta precision of \citet{janicki2020properties}, evaluated at the model mean in a second stage, and Gamma CV matching---with default priors chosen by simulation (Table \ref{tab:priors}), achieving $117\times$ to $530\times$ speedups over Stan-based MCMC at the largest benchmarked sizes while maintaining near-perfect inferential concordance ($r = 0.9993$).
  \item \textbf{Two-Stage Hierarchical Benchmarking Engine}: We implement a modular calibration engine (\code{benchmark\_sae()}) that simultaneously enforces consistency across multi-level administrative hierarchies (Level 0 National, Level 1 Provincial, Level 2 District) via ratio, difference, variance-weighted optimal, and logit-scale adjustments.
  \item \textbf{End-to-End Official Statistics Dissemination Suite}: We provide turnkey post-estimation workflows, including automated reliability tagging (\code{add\_reliability\_flags()}) based on official NSO thresholds, model concordance diagnostics (\code{compare\_sae()}), publication-ready choropleth mapping (\code{map\_sae()}), and multi-sheet spreadsheet exports (\code{export\_sae()}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodological framework} \label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Frequentist small area models} \label{sec:methods_freq}

\subsubsection{Classical Fay-Herriot model}

Let \(m\) denote the number of small areas indexed by \(i = 1, \dots, m\). The classical area-level model \citep{fay1979estimates} is defined by:
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_model}
y_i = \bm{x}_i^\top \bm{\beta} + u_i + e_i, \quad u_i \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2), \quad e_i \overset{\mathrm{ind}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, D_i),
\end{equation}
\%
where \(y_i\) is the design-based direct estimator of the target indicator \(\theta_i\), \(\bm{x}_i\) is a \(p \times 1\) vector of auxiliary covariates, \(D_i\) is the known design variance, and \(u_i\) is the area random effect. The Empirical Best Linear Unbiased Predictor (EBLUP) is given by:
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_eblup}
\hat{\theta}_i = \hat{\gamma}_i y_i + (1 - \hat{\gamma}_i) \bm{x}_i^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}}, \quad \hat{\gamma}_i = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_u^2}{\hat{\sigma}_u^2 + D_i},
\end{equation}
\%
where \(\hat{\bm{\beta}} = (\bm{X}^\top \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} \bm{X})^{-1} \bm{X}^\top \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} \bm{y}\) with \(\bm{V} = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_u^2 + D_1, \dots, \sigma_u^2 + D_m)\). Mean squared error is evaluated via the second-order Prasad-Rao approximation \citep{prasad1990estimation}:
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:fh_mse}
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_i) \approx g_{1i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2) + g_{2i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2) + 2 g_{3i}(\hat{\sigma}_u^2).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Spatial Fay-Herriot model}

To accommodate spatial dependence across contiguous domains, \citet{petrucci2006small} and \citet{pratesi2008small} modeled the area random effects \(\bm{u} = (u_1, \dots, u_m)^\top\) via a Simultaneous Autoregressive (SAR) process:
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:sfh_sar}
\bm{u} = \rho \bm{W} \bm{u} + \bm{\epsilon}, \quad \bm{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}_m(\bm{0}, \sigma_1^2 \bm{I}_m),
\end{equation}
\%
where \(\rho\) is the spatial autoregressive parameter and \(\bm{W}\) is a row-standardized spatial proximity matrix. The marginal covariance matrix is \(\bm{V}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) = \bm{G}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) + \bm{D}\), where \(\bm{G}(\sigma_1^2, \rho) = \sigma_1^2 [(\bm{I}_m - \rho \bm{W})^\top (\bm{I}_m - \rho \bm{W})]^{-1}\). The spatial EBLUP is:
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:sfh_eblup}
\hat{\bm{\theta}} = \bm{X} \hat{\bm{\beta}} + \hat{\bm{G}} \hat{\bm{V}}^{-1} (\bm{y} - \bm{X} \hat{\bm{\beta}}).
\end{equation}
\%
The SAR process is defined on all domains of \(\bm{W}\), including unsampled ones, which are predicted by spatial kriging. Variance components \((\sigma_1^2, \rho)\) are estimated by REML or ML Fisher scoring with \(|\rho| \le 0.999\), falling back to a constrained (projected) Fisher scoring when the classical iteration ends at a negative variance. All quantities are obtained from sparse Cholesky factorizations of \(\bm{A} = (\bm{I} - \rho\bm{W})^\top(\bm{I} - \rho\bm{W})\) and \(\bm{A} + \sigma_1^2 \bm{E}^\top \bm{D}^{-1} \bm{E}\) through the Woodbury identity, so no dense \(m \times m\) inverse is formed. Mean squared error uses the same second-order approximation \(g_1 + g_2 + 2g_3 - g_4\) as \CRANpkg{sae} \citep{molina2015sae}, or parametric and non-parametric bootstrap.

\subsubsection{Spatio-temporal Fay-Herriot model}

When small area data are collected across longitudinal waves \(t = 1, \dots, T\), borrowing strength across space and time yields substantial efficiency gains \citep{marhuenda2013small}:
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:stfh_model}
y_{it} = \bm{x}_{it}^\top \bm{\beta} + u_{1,it} + u_{2,it} + e_{it},
\end{equation}
\%
where \(\bm{u}_1 = \rho_1 \bm{W} \bm{u}_1 + \bm{\epsilon}_1\) is a spatial SAR effect that is constant over time within each area (\(u_{1,it} = u_{1,i}\)) and \(u_{2,it} = \rho_2 u_{2,i,t-1} + \epsilon_{2,it}\) follows an AR(1) temporal process with \(|\rho_2| < 1\), as in \citet{marhuenda2013small}. In \CRANpkg{fastsae}, the covariance \(\bm{V} = \bm{A} + \bm{Z}\bm{V}_{u_1}\bm{Z}^\top\) (with \(\bm{A}\) block diagonal in \(T \times T\) blocks) is never formed: REML scores, Fisher information, and the analytical MSE (\(g_1 + g_2 + 2g_3 - g_4\)) are computed from \(T \times T\), \(D \times D\), and low-rank \(DT \times (D + p)\) pieces via the Woodbury identity. The panel need not be complete: the model is defined on the full \(D \times T\) grid, the variance components are estimated by REML from the sampled cells, and missing periods as well as areas that are never sampled are predicted by the best linear unbiased predictor given the sampled data \citep{saei2005out}, with analytical (second-order) and parametric bootstrap MSE for every cell.

\subsubsection{Unit-level Battese-Harter-Fuller model}

When unit microdata are accessible alongside population auxiliary totals, \CRANpkg{fastsae} implements the nested error regression model \citep{battese1988error}:
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bhf_model}
y_{dj} = \bm{x}_{dj}^\top \bm{\beta} + u_d + e_{dj}, \quad d = 1, \dots, D, \quad j = 1, \dots, n_d,
\end{equation}
\%
yielding EBLUP predictors \(\hat{\bar{Y}}_d = f_d \bar{y}_d + (1 - f_d)(\bar{\bm{X}}_{d, r}^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}} + \hat{\gamma}_d (\bar{y}_d - \bar{\bm{x}}_d^\top \hat{\bm{\beta}}))\), where \(f_d = n_d / N_d\).

\subsection{Hierarchical Bayes models} \label{sec:methods_inla}

\subsubsection{Latent Gaussian models}

The Bayesian functions formulate small area estimation as a latent Gaussian model (LGM) \citep{rue2009approximate}. For the area-level model of \code{hb\_area()},
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:lgm_formulation}
y_i \mid \mu_i, \psi_i \sim \pi(y_i \mid \mu_i, \psi_i), \quad \eta_i = g(\mu_i) = \bm{x}_i^\top \bm{\beta} + u_i,
\end{equation}
\%
where \(g(\cdot)\) is the canonical link of the family (identity, logit or log), \(\psi_i\) a dispersion parameter (described below) and \(\bm{u}\) a zero-mean Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF): independent effects \(u_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2)\) or one of the spatial structures below, with temporal and space-time terms added for panel data. \code{hb\_unit()} applies the same construction to unit records with a domain effect, and \code{hb\_twofold()} to sub-areas nested in areas, with an area effect \(v_i\) and an independent sub-area effect \(u_{ij}\). INLA approximates the posterior marginals of the area parameters \(\mu_i\) by nested Laplace approximations and numerical integration over the hyperparameters; \CRANpkg{fastsae} uses the full Laplace approximation (\code{strategy = "laplace"}) by default, which is more accurate than the simplified Laplace approximation for non-Gaussian likelihoods with little information per area.

\subsubsection{Survey dispersion and variance matching} \label{sec:methods_dispersion}

To preserve the design-based sampling variances \(D_i\) of the direct estimates, \CRANpkg{fastsae} fixes the dispersion parameter of each area at the value for which the model variance equals \(D_i\):
\%

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Gaussian}: the precision is fixed to the inverse design variance, $\tau_i = 1/D_i$, which gives the Fay-Herriot model.
  \item \textbf{Beta}: for proportions $y_i \in (0, 1)$ with logit link, $\mathrm{Var}(y_i) = \mu_i(1 - \mu_i)/(1 + \phi_i)$. Equating this to $D_i$ gives the precision of \citet{janicki2020properties},
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:beta_dispersion}
  \phi_i = \max\left( \frac{\mu_i (1 - \mu_i)}{D_i} - 1, \, 1 \right).
  \end{equation}
  It depends on the unknown $\mu_i$. Replacing $\mu_i$ by the direct estimate $y_i$ makes the model variance $D_i \mu_i(1 - \mu_i)/\{y_i(1 - y_i)\}$, too small wherever $y_i$ falls far from $\mu_i$, and in a simulation with proportions near 0.08 the 95\% intervals covered only 81--84\% of the true values. \CRANpkg{fastsae} therefore fits the model twice and evaluates (\ref{eq:beta_dispersion}) at the posterior means of the first fit, which restored a coverage of 90--94\%. With known sample sizes $n_i$ instead of $D_i$, $\phi_i = n_i - 1$.
  \item \textbf{Gamma}: for positive skewed indicators with log link, equating $\mathrm{Var}(y_i) = \mu_i^2 / s_i$ to $D_i$ gives the shape $s_i = y_i^2/D_i = 1/\mathrm{CV}_i^2$.
  \item \textbf{Negative Binomial}: equating $\mathrm{Var}(y_i) = \mu_i + \mu_i^2/k_i$ to $D_i$ gives the size $k_i = \hat\mu_i^2/(D_i - \hat\mu_i)$, again at the posterior means $\hat\mu_i$ of a first fit.
  \item \textbf{Binomial and Poisson}: the domain sample sizes $n_i$ are the numbers of trials, and population exposures $E_i$ enter as the offset $\log(E_i)$.
\end{enumerate}

\%
Without sampling variances, a single dispersion parameter is estimated. With one direct estimate per area it trades off against the area effect, and its prior then determines the intervals (Table \ref{tab:priors}); \code{hb\_area()} warns in this case.

\subsubsection{Spatial priors and space-time interactions}

The area effect \(\bm{u}\) supports five graph-based structures: the intrinsic CAR (ICAR) model of \citet{besag1991bayesian}, the BYM model with independent and ICAR components, the scaled BYM2 model of \citet{riebler2016intuitive}, \(u_i = \sigma_u(\sqrt{1 - \phi}\, v_i + \sqrt{\phi}\, u^*_i)\) with \(u^*\) a scaled ICAR field, the Leroux CAR with precision \(\bm{Q} = \tau[(1 - \lambda)\bm{I} + \lambda(\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}) - \bm{W})]\) \citep{leroux2000estimation}, and simultaneous autoregressive models, either for the area effect, \(\bm{u} = (\bm{I} - \rho\bm{W})^{-1}\bm{\varepsilon}\) as in the spatial Fay-Herriot model, or as a spatial lag model (SLM). For panel observations across \(T \ge 2\), temporal trends support random walks (RW1, RW2), AR(1) and independent effects. Space-time interactions can be specified as domain-specific trends, a separable spatial field with AR(1) dynamics, or according to the four \citet{knorrheld2000bayesian} interaction types (\(\bm{Q}_{st} = \bm{Q}_s \otimes \bm{Q}_t\) for type IV).

\subsubsection{Default prior distributions} \label{sec:methods_priors}

With few areas, or one observation per area, the data hardly inform the variance and dispersion parameters, and their priors decide how strongly the estimates are shrunk and how wide the intervals are. Table \ref{tab:priors} lists the defaults, which are shared by the INLA and JAX engines so that both fit the same model.

The half-normal prior is the weakly informative choice of \citet{gelman2006prior} for hierarchical standard deviations. Its scale is tied to the response: a penalized complexity (PC) prior \(P(\sigma > U) = \alpha\) \citep{simpson2017penalising} is a good default when \(U\) is chosen for the problem, but the common \(U = 1\), \(\alpha = 0.01\) depends on the unit of \(y\). In a Fay-Herriot simulation with 30 areas the 95\% intervals under PC\((1, 0.01)\) covered 94\%, 85\% and 38\% of the true means for the same data expressed in three units, while the scaled half-normal prior covered as the EBLUP intervals (93--97\% for 15 to 60 areas, EBLUP 93--96\%), and PC priors with \(U = \mathrm{sd}(\bm{y})\) still covered only 85--94\% because they put much mass near \(\sigma = 0\). On the logit and log scales, half-normal\((1)\) covered at least as well as half-normal\((2.5)\), the default of \CRANpkg{tipsae}, and estimated the area SD with less bias. For the dispersion parameters estimated without \(D_i\), INLA's own defaults put the Gamma shape near 80 and the Negative Binomial size near 110--180 whatever the truth, and intervals for overdispersed data then covered 52--92\% of the true means; the half-normal\((10)\) prior covered 90--99\%, at the cost of too wide intervals when the sampling noise is small, which is why \(D_i\) should be supplied whenever it is known. The simulations and the code behind them are documented in the package vignette \code{vignette("hb-priors")}.

\subsection{Two-stage hierarchical benchmarking calibration} \label{sec:methods_bench}

In official statistics, model-based small area estimates \(\hat{\theta}_{di}\) for domain \(i\) in province/region \(g\) must aggregate to officially published higher-level targets \citep{rao2015small, stefan2021benchmarked}. Let \(w_{di}\) denote domain population weights such that \(W_g = \sum_{i \in g} w_{di}\) is the provincial total weight, and \(W_{\text{nat}} = \sum_g W_g\) is the national total weight. Let \(T_g\) denote the provincial target and \(T_{\text{nat}}\) denote the national aggregate target.

\CRANpkg{fastsae} implements a two-stage hierarchical benchmarking calibration:
\%

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Stage 1: Harmonization of Provincial Targets to National Benchmark}: When initial provincial targets $T_g$ (or initial aggregated model estimates $\hat{\bar{\theta}}_g = \sum_{i \in g} (w_{di}/W_g)\hat{\theta}_{di}$) do not sum to the national target $T_{\text{nat}}$, calibrated provincial targets $\tilde{T}_g$ are computed to satisfy $\sum_g (W_g / W_{\text{nat}}) \tilde{T}_g = T_{\text{nat}}$.
  \item \textbf{Stage 2: Domain-Level Calibration within Provinces}: Domain estimates within province $g$ are calibrated to satisfy $\sum_{i \in g} (w_{di}/W_g) \hat{\theta}_{di}^{\text{BM}} = \tilde{T}_g$.
\end{enumerate}

\%
Across both stages, \CRANpkg{fastsae} supports four calibration mechanisms:
\%

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ratio Benchmarking} \citep{you2002small}: $\hat{\theta}_{di}^{\text{BM}} = \hat{\theta}_{di} \left( \tilde{T}_g / \sum_{j \in g} \tilde{w}_{dj} \hat{\theta}_{dj} \right)$.
  \item \textbf{Difference Benchmarking} \citep{rao2015small}: $\hat{\theta}_{di}^{\text{BM}} = \hat{\theta}_{di} + \left( \tilde{T}_g - \sum_{j \in g} \tilde{w}_{dj} \hat{\theta}_{dj} \right)$.
  \item \textbf{Variance-Weighted Optimal Benchmarking} \citep{datta2011bayesian, bell2013benchmarking}: Adjustments minimize $\sum_{i} (\hat{\theta}_{di}^{\text{BM}} - \hat{\theta}_{di})^2 / \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_{di})$ subject to the benchmark constraint, which allocates them in proportion to $\tilde{w}_{di}\,\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_{di})$:
  \begin{equation}
  \hat{\theta}_{di}^{\text{BM}} = \hat{\theta}_{di} + \tilde{w}_{di} \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_{di}) \left( \sum_{j \in g} \tilde{w}_{dj}^2 \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_{dj}) \right)^{-1} \left( \tilde{T}_g - \sum_{j \in g} \tilde{w}_{dj} \hat{\theta}_{dj} \right).
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Logit-Scale Benchmarking}: For bounded proportions in $(0, 1)$, linear adjustments can push estimates outside $[0, 1]$. \CRANpkg{fastsae} solves for an additive shift $\alpha_g$ on the logit scale:
  \begin{equation}
  \sum_{i \in g} \tilde{w}_{di} \, \mathrm{logit}^{-1}\left( \mathrm{logit}(\hat{\theta}_{di}) + \alpha_g \right) = \tilde{T}_g,
  \end{equation}
  via robust one-dimensional root-finding, guaranteeing $\hat{\theta}_{di}^{\text{BM}} \in (0, 1)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Official statistics reliability classification} \label{sec:methods_reliability}

NSOs classify domain estimates into publication tiers based on the Relative Standard Error (\(\mathrm{RSE} = \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_i)}}{|\hat{\theta}_i|} \times 100\%\)). \CRANpkg{fastsae} provides \code{add\_reliability\_flags()} implementing institutional thresholding:
\%
\begin{equation} \label{eq:reliability}
\mathrm{Tier}_i = \begin{cases}
\text{Reliable (Direct Publish)}, & \mathrm{RSE}_i \le c_1, \\
\text{Use with Caution (Accompanying Warning)}, & c_1 < \mathrm{RSE}_i \le c_2, \\
\text{Unreliable (Suppress or Aggregate)}, & \mathrm{RSE}_i > c_2,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\%
where the default thresholds \((c_1, c_2) = (25\%, 50\%)\) follow the criteria of BPS-Statistics Indonesia (with \(\mathrm{RSE} \le c_1\) reliable and \(\mathrm{RSE} > c_2\) unreliable); other agencies' cut-offs can be supplied.

The frequentist engines (\code{eblup\_fh()}, \code{eblup\_sfh()}, \code{eblup\_stfh()}, \code{eblup\_bhf()}, \code{eblup\_twofold()}) never form a dense inverse of the marginal covariance: the spatial model works with sparse Cholesky factors (\CRANpkg{RcppEigen}), the spatio-temporal and two-fold models with block-structured Woodbury identities, and the unit-level model with domain-level sufficient statistics, profiling \(\bm{\beta}\) and \(\sigma_e^2\) out of the (restricted) likelihood. The bootstrap MSE loops run in parallel with OpenMP (\code{\#pragma omp parallel for}); random numbers are drawn sequentially from R's generator beforehand, so results depend only on the seed and not on the number of threads. The Bayesian functions (\code{hb\_area()}, \code{hb\_unit()}, \code{hb\_twofold()}) translate formulas, spatial adjacency matrices \(\bm{W}\), and survey dispersion arguments into \pkg{R-INLA} calls. How a model is estimated is specified separately from the model by an engine object: \code{inla\_engine()} holds the INLA strategy, priors, and further \code{INLA::inla()} arguments, and \code{jax\_engine()} fits \code{hb\_area()} models by NUTS sampling in JAX/NumPyro (CPU or GPU) through \CRANpkg{reticulate}. Standard S3 methods (\code{print()}, \code{summary()}, \code{plot()}, \code{coef()}, \code{fitted()}, \code{residuals()}) are implemented across all model classes.

\section{Applied illustration and production workflow} \label{sec:applications}

We demonstrate an end-to-end NSO production workflow using the benchmark dataset \code{sim\_area} (\(D = 42\) domains across 6 provincial groups).

\subsection{Loading data, spatial weights, and fitting models}

\begin{verbatim}
library("fastsae")
data("sim_area")
sim_area$prov <- rep(paste0("Prov_", 1:6), each = 7)
sim_area$pop <- round(runif(nrow(sim_area), 15000, 45000))

# Construct row-standardized spatial proximity matrix
coords <- cbind(sim_area$x_coord, sim_area$y_coord)
dists <- as.matrix(dist(coords))
W <- (dists < quantile(dists, 0.18)) * 1
diag(W) <- 0
rs <- rowSums(W); rs[rs == 0] <- 1; W_std <- W / rs

# Fit Frequentist and Bayesian Models
fit_fh <- eblup_fh(y_gaussian ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  data = sim_area, method = "REML", print_result = FALSE)
fit_sfh <- eblup_sfh(y_gaussian ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  domain = "area", W = W_std, data = sim_area,
  method = "REML", print_result = FALSE)
fit_beta <- hb_area(y_beta ~ x1 + x2, vardir = "vardir",
  data = sim_area, family = "beta", spatial = "bym2",
  W = W, print_result = FALSE)
\end{verbatim}

Future developments will focus on expanding unit-level spatial EBP under non-Gaussian likelihoods, integrating zero-inflated Beta (ZIB) specifications for extreme sparse indicators, and supporting direct spatial polygon streaming.
